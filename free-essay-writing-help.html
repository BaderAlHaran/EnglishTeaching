Free Essay Writing Help

Practical advice for the essay you are working on right now.

Start with a structure

Grammar is rarely what costs you marks. Usually it is the reader losing track of your argument. Sorting the structure out first prevents most of that.

  • Introduction. A sentence or two of context, then your thesis. Don’t save the argument for the end.
  • Body paragraphs. One idea each. If a paragraph is doing two jobs, split it.
  • Counter-argument. Name the strongest objection to your thesis and answer it. This is often what lifts an essay above average.
  • Conclusion. Say what your argument establishes. No new evidence here.

Here is a quick test. Copy out your thesis and every topic sentence on their own, in order. If that list still reads like an argument, your structure works.

Write a thesis that argues something

A thesis isn’t a description of your topic. It’s the claim your essay defends, and someone should be able to disagree with it.

Too weak: “This essay will discuss the effects of social media on students.” That just announces a topic. There is nothing there to disagree with.

Stronger: “Social media harms student performance less through distraction than through disrupted sleep, which means device bans during class miss the real problem.” That one is arguable, and it tells the reader what the essay has to prove.

If you can’t imagine anyone reasonable objecting to it, you have probably written a fact rather than an argument.

Make every paragraph do its job

The most common problem is a quotation that never gets explained. A quotation doesn’t argue for you. You have to say what it shows.

  • Topic sentence. The claim this paragraph proves, in your own words.
  • Evidence. The quotation, the data, the example.
  • Explanation. Why that evidence supports the claim. This should be the longest part. It is almost always the part that gets rushed.
  • Link. How this connects back to your thesis.

If a paragraph ends right after a quotation, the explanation is missing.

Fix the mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Sentences that never end. If a sentence runs past about 30 words, it usually contains two sentences. Split it.
  • Hedging. “It could perhaps be argued that there may be some evidence” says nothing at all. Just make the claim.
  • Filler openers. “Since the dawn of time” and “In today’s society” tell the reader nothing. Start with your actual point.
  • Inconsistent citations. Pick the style your course requires and apply it identically every time.
  • Answering a different question. Reread the prompt after your final draft. Check it clause by clause, because it is easy to drift.

Check it before you submit

Work through this list on your final draft:

  • Does the introduction state an arguable thesis?
  • Does each paragraph stick to one idea?
  • Is every quotation actually explained?
  • Have you addressed the strongest objection to your argument?
  • Are the citations complete and consistent?
  • Have you read it aloud? You will hear problems you can’t see.
  • Have you checked spelling, grammar, and sentence length?

A checker catches mechanics. It won’t notice that your third paragraph contradicts your thesis, or that your conclusion answers a question you never actually asked.

Using writing help honestly

Everything on this page is meant to improve writing you did yourself. We don’t write essays, and we won’t produce work for you to hand in as your own. Our FAQ spells out exactly where the line is.

Most institutions allow help with grammar, clarity, and structure, but not with content someone else wrote. The rules do vary, so check your own institution’s policy.
