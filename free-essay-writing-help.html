Free Essay Writing Help

Practical guidance you can apply to the draft in front of you — how to structure it, how to sharpen your thesis, and what to check before you hand it in.

Start with a structure

Most essays that lose marks do not lose them for grammar. They lose them because the reader cannot follow the argument. A simple, reliable structure fixes most of that before you write a word.

  • Introduction — give the context in a sentence or two, then state your thesis. Do not save your argument for the end.
  • Body paragraphs — one idea each. If a paragraph is doing two jobs, split it.
  • Counter-argument — name the strongest objection to your thesis and answer it. This is often what separates a good essay from an average one.
  • Conclusion — say what your argument establishes. Do not introduce new evidence here.

A useful test: write out your thesis and each topic sentence on their own, in order. If that list alone reads as a coherent argument, your structure is sound.

Write a thesis that argues something

A thesis is not a description of your topic. It is the claim your essay defends — and someone should be able to disagree with it.

Too weak: “This essay will discuss the effects of social media on students.” That announces a topic. Nobody can disagree with it.

Stronger: “Social media harms student performance less through distraction than through disrupted sleep, which means device bans during class miss the real problem.” That is specific, arguable, and it tells the reader exactly what the essay has to prove.

If you cannot imagine a reasonable person objecting to your thesis, it is probably a statement of fact rather than an argument.

Make every paragraph do its job

The most common paragraph problem is evidence that is quoted but never explained. A quotation does not argue for you — you have to say what it shows.

  • Topic sentence — the claim this paragraph proves, in your own words.
  • Evidence — the quotation, data, or example.
  • Explanation — why that evidence supports the claim. This is usually the longest part, and usually the part that gets cut short.
  • Link — how this connects back to your thesis.

If a paragraph ends immediately after a quotation, that is a reliable sign the explanation is missing.

Fix the mistakes that cost the most marks

  • Sentences that never end. If a sentence runs past about 30 words, it usually contains two sentences. Split it.
  • Hedging. “It could perhaps be argued that there may be some evidence” says nothing. Make the claim.
  • Filler openers. “Since the dawn of time” and “In today’s society” carry no information. Start with your actual point.
  • Inconsistent citations. Pick the style your course requires and apply it identically every time.
  • Answering a different question. Reread the prompt after your final draft and check, clause by clause, that you addressed all of it.

Check it before you submit

Work through this list on your final draft:

  • Does the introduction state an arguable thesis?
  • Does every paragraph carry one clear idea and a topic sentence?
  • Is every piece of evidence explained, not just quoted?
  • Have you addressed the strongest objection to your argument?
  • Are the citations complete and consistent?
  • Have you read it aloud? You will hear problems you cannot see.
  • Have you checked spelling, grammar, and sentence length?

When you want a person to read it

Automated checking is good at mechanics and blind to meaning. It cannot tell you that your third paragraph contradicts your thesis, that your evidence does not prove what you claim, or that your conclusion answers a question you never asked.

For work that matters — a personal statement, an IELTS or TOEFL essay, a thesis chapter — a real reader is worth it. Using writing help honestly

Everything on this page is meant to improve writing you did yourself. We do not write essays, and we will not produce work for you to submit as your own — our FAQ sets out exactly what we do and do not do.

Most institutions allow help with grammar, clarity, and structure, but not with content someone else authored. Check your own institution’s academic integrity policy, since the rules differ. The principle that keeps you safe is simple: the ideas, the argument, and the words should be yours, and help should make your work clearer rather than replace it.
